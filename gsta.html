P-04 · Survey research · Professional development

What Psychology Graduate Students Said About Teaching Support

I contributed to the analysis of a survey that asked psychology graduate students about their teaching experiences, awareness of teaching-focused professional resources, and the support they felt when considering a teaching-focused career.

The GSTA team published the original summary of the survey findings.

Read the full GSTA analysis →

A note on this page: What follows is my own retelling and reflection on the project. It is not intended to reproduce the GSTA team's report or to speak on behalf of the Society for the Teaching of Psychology or the Graduate Student Teaching Association.

I focus on the findings that stood out to me while working with the data, the questions they raised for me, and some possible ways I think about their practical meaning. Those interpretations are my own.

I also think the findings should be taken with some perspective. Fifty-seven responses can reveal interesting patterns and document that different engagement experiences exist, but they cannot tell us what all psychology graduate students experience. I see this survey as a useful snapshot and a informational starting point for better questions rather than a final answer.

Project type
Graduate student survey
Responses
57 psychology graduate students
Focus
Teaching experiences, professional awareness, and career support
My role
Data analysis and interpretation

01 · Why this interested me

Graduate students can spend years preparing to teach without necessarily knowing what teaching resources are available to them.

Graduate training tends to make research opportunities fairly visible. Students hear about laboratories, conferences, publications, grants, and research mentorship because those activities are built into many graduate programs.

Teaching development can feel less obvious. A graduate student may be teaching a course, working as a teaching assistant, or considering a teaching-focused career while knowing relatively little about the organizations, conferences, funding, and professional communities built around teaching.

That was one reason I found this survey interesting. Instead of assuming that students knew where those resources were, the survey gave us a chance to ask what students had actually heard about and how supported they felt.

From my perspective, this makes the project partly a question about information access. An opportunity can exist and still have very little practical value to someone who never learns about it.

02 · Who responded

A small snapshot of psychology graduate students

The survey received 57 responses. Most respondents were located in the United States, with a smaller group located in Canada. Most were enrolled in PhD programs, while others reported being in joint master's and PhD programs, master's programs, or PsyD programs.

93%

Located in the United States

Most respondents were located in the United States. The remaining 7% were located in Canada.

70%

Enrolled in PhD programs

Most respondents identified their current program as a PhD program. Other respondents were enrolled in joint master's and PhD, master's, or PsyD programs.

The survey did not require respondents to provide detailed demographic information. However, 46% voluntarily shared information about aspects of their identity.

Among those responses were students who identified as BIPOC, first-generation, LGBTQIA+, low-income, disabled or neurodivergent, or nontraditional age students. Some respondents described more than one identity.

I think this information is useful for understanding who was represented in the responses, but I would not treat it as a demographic profile of psychology graduate students more generally. Participation was voluntary, and the sample was small.

03 · What stood out to me

Many respondents had not heard about major teaching resources.

The awareness results were probably the part of the survey that caught my attention most. They made me think about the difference between creating a professional opportunity and actually getting that opportunity in front of the people who might use it.

59%

Had not previously heard of STP

More than half of respondents indicated that they had not heard about the Society for the Teaching of Psychology before participating in the survey.

81%

Had not known about ACT

A large majority reported that they had not previously known about the Annual Conference on Teaching.

My takeaway

What struck me was not simply that some students did not know about these resources. It was how easy that information gap could be to miss.

From the perspective of an organization or department, a conference, travel award, teaching resource, or professional community may already be available. From the perspective of a student who has never heard about it, however, that opportunity effectively does not exist yet.

It made me think about access as partly a communication problem. Sometimes improving support may not require creating something completely new. It may begin with asking whether existing resources are reaching students through the people and channels they actually pay attention to.

04 · Support for teaching careers

Respondents reported stronger support from advisors than from departments.

The survey also asked students how supported they felt when considering a teaching-focused career after graduate school.

Fifty-nine percent indicated that they felt very supported by their advisor. In comparison, 30% indicated that they felt very supported by their department.

I would not interpret this as evidence that departments were generally unsupportive. The survey measured students' perceptions, and we do not have enough information here to know why students rated advisors and departments differently.

What I do think the pattern raises is a useful question. How much does a graduate student's exposure to teaching careers depend on having an individual advisor who happens to talk about and support those careers?

If that support varies mainly from advisor to advisor, students within the same department could end up receiving very different information about teaching opportunities.

That possibility is interesting to me because it shifts the question from only individual mentorship to the environment surrounding the student. A supportive mentor matters, but a program can also make information visible in ways that do not depend on one person's mentoring style.

05 · My role

Turning survey responses into a clearer picture

My contribution to this project was primarily on the analysis side. I worked with Josh Lovett on the survey data and helped summarize patterns that could be communicated clearly to a broader audience.

  • Reviewed the survey response data and helped organize information for descriptive analysis.
  • Co-analyzed patterns related to respondents' graduate programs, awareness of teaching resources, and perceived professional support.
  • Helped identify quantitative patterns that could be communicated in the public GSTA survey synopsis.
  • Considered how to summarize the findings without treating a small voluntary sample as representative of all psychology graduate students.

The original survey was created by Lauren Girouard-Hallam. Data analysis was conducted by Josh Lovett and me. The published synopsis was written by Alexa Sacchi. The published graphics were created using Canva.

06 · Questions the findings raised for me

What would I want to understand next?

The survey identifies patterns, but it does not tell us exactly what produced them. For me, that is where the more interesting follow-up questions begin.

Where do students actually get information?

I would want to know which channels students actually notice. Is information more likely to reach them through advisors, department emails, other graduate students, orientation, teaching centers, social media, or professional organizations?

When does the information become useful?

A first year graduate student may need something different from a student preparing to teach independently or entering the job market. Timing may matter as much as whether the information was technically available.

How much does support depend on the advisor?

I would be interested in whether students receive similar career information across a department or whether access depends heavily on the interests and knowledge of an individual mentor.

Does awareness turn into participation?

Knowing that a conference or resource exists is only one step. A useful follow-up would examine whether students use those resources, what prevents participation, and which resources they actually find valuable.

07 · What I took from the project

Simple descriptive data can still reveal an important practical question.

This was a much smaller project than some of my experimental research, but I found it useful for a different reason.

It reminded me that not every useful research question requires a complicated statistical model. Sometimes a straightforward survey can reveal a mismatch between what an organization or institution provides and what people actually know is available.

At the same time, descriptive patterns leave a lot unanswered. These data cannot tell us why a particular student had not heard about STP or ACT. They cannot tell us whether greater awareness would change career decisions. They also cannot tell us whether the same patterns would appear in a larger or more representative sample.

I think that combination is useful. The survey gives enough information to identify a possible problem, but not enough to pretend that we already understand the mechanism behind it.

Availability is not the same as access. An opportunity becomes useful only after people know it exists and can realistically see themselves using it.

That idea is what stayed with me most. When thinking about professional development, I now pay more attention not only to whether resources exist, but also to how information moves through an environment and who may or may not encounter it.

08 · A grain of salt

How I would interpret these findings cautiously

I think this is an important part of presenting the project. The survey included 57 respondents. It was not designed as a probability sample of all psychology graduate students.

That means I would not use percentages such as 59% or 81% to claim that the same percentages apply to psychology graduate students nationally.

I would instead describe the results as patterns within this particular group of respondents. Those patterns are interesting because they point toward questions worth investigating on a larger scale.

The survey also measured reported awareness and perceived support. It does not establish what caused those experiences. For example, lower departmental support could reflect communication, program priorities, available teaching opportunities, student expectations, or factors that were not measured in this survey.

In other words, I think the findings are meaningful, but I would treat them as an invitation to investigate further rather than as a definitive diagnosis of graduate teaching support.

09 · If I continued the research

Moving from a snapshot toward explaining the pattern

A larger follow-up study could recruit students from a wider range of institutions and examine not only whether students know about teaching resources, but how they first encountered them.

I would also want to measure characteristics of the surrounding graduate environment. For example, does the department regularly circulate teaching opportunities? Are teaching careers discussed during professional development? Do faculty mentors vary in how often they discuss teaching?

From there, an intervention could test something concrete. A department might introduce a structured teaching-resource briefing or regular communication system and examine whether awareness, participation, or perceived support changes afterward.

That progression is what interests me: first identify the pattern, then understand the conditions associated with it, and finally test whether changing those conditions changes the outcome.
