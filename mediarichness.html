Case study 01 · Experimental research

How Communication Format Shapes Engagement and Trust

A controlled remote study examining whether receiving the same message through live video or voice-only communication changes belief revision, active engagement, and behavioral reliance.

Role
Principal investigator and lead researcher
Timeline
2022–2026
Participants
62 children: 31 five-year-olds and 31 seven-year-olds
Methods
Experiment, behavioral observation, GLMMs, time-to-event analysis

01 · The problem

The same words do not always create the same experience.

Communication platforms often allow the same conversation to happen through video or voice only. Although the words may remain identical, removing video also removes eye gaze, facial expression, gesture, and other cues that help people interpret whether a speaker is addressing them and engaging with them in real time.

Research frequently treats channels as interchangeable delivery systems. I wanted to test whether the channel itself changes how a person responds to and relies on the speaker.

When message content is held constant, does the communication channel change how children respond to a speaker?

02 · Research questions

Trust was treated as a set of behaviors—not one score.

  1. Would children be more willing to revise an existing belief after receiving counterintuitive testimony through video rather than voice only?
  2. Would communication format affect spontaneous protest—the tendency to interrupt, object, or correct a speaker?
  3. Would communication format affect how long children waited after the speaker said she would return with a delayed benefit?

Together, these outcomes captured information use, active engagement, and behavioral reliance within the same interaction.

03 · Study design

Isolating the effect of communication format

Children were randomly assigned to interact with the same researcher through either live video or live voice chat. I held the message content, study sequence, speaker, and behavioral tasks constant so differences could be attributed more confidently to the communication format.

During a learning task, the researcher made claims that contradicted familiar categories. Children could maintain their initial answer or revise it to match the speaker, and I recorded whether they spontaneously protested against the claims.

The interaction then shifted from informational trust to behavioral reliance. The researcher promised to return with a delayed benefit. Children could wait up to eight minutes or end the wait early, producing a time-to-event outcome rather than a simple yes/no response.

04 · My role

Leading the full research lifecycle

  • Defined the research questions and experimental comparison.
  • Developed child-friendly, standardized materials for remote use.
  • Coordinated recruitment through community outreach, ResearchMatch, and referrals.
  • Created procedures for consent, assent, withdrawal, and secure data handling.
  • Trained research support and monitored protocol fidelity.
  • Built trial-level datasets and data-quality procedures.
  • Selected analyses suited to repeated binary decisions and censored waiting-time data.
  • Interpreted the findings and developed conference and manuscript outputs.

05 · Analysis

Choosing models that matched the decisions

A single summary score would have hidden when and how children changed their responses. I analyzed trial-level outcomes using generalized mixed-effects models, accounting for repeated responses from the same participant.

Waiting posed a different problem: some children completed the full interval, so their exact stopping time was not observed. I used time-to-event analysis so those full waits were handled appropriately. Because the younger subgroup was modest and some outcomes were sparse, I used Firth-penalized models to reduce small-sample bias.

06 · Key findings

Video changed several forms of engagement.

9.66× Higher odds of belief revision

Children in the video condition had substantially higher odds of endorsing the speaker’s counterintuitive claims than children in voice only.

4.22× Higher odds of spontaneous protest

Video also increased objections and corrections, suggesting active engagement with the speaker rather than passive compliance.

91% Lower risk of ending the wait early

Among five-year-olds, the estimated hazard of stopping was lower after video. Seven-year-olds showed a ceiling effect across conditions.

A communication channel does more than carry content. It can change how strongly people engage with, interpret, and rely on the person delivering that content.

07 · Theoretical implication

Engagement can include both challenge and acceptance.

Greater protest and greater belief revision may look contradictory if trust is treated as simple agreement. Together, they suggest that visible cues increased active engagement: children were more likely to challenge the claim in the moment and still more willing to use the speaker’s information later.

Theoretically, the study supports a multidimensional account of trust. Protest, belief revision, and persistence reflect distinct responses to the same source and should not be collapsed into one measure.

08 · Applied translation

What this evidence could help teams decide

This was an academic study, not a commercial product evaluation. These are research-informed applications to test—not claims of achieved product impact.

Product & UX design

Test when visible social cues improve onboarding, remote support, tutoring, or other moments when users must interpret unfamiliar information or rely on a representative. Do not assume an identical script creates an equivalent experience across channels.

Intervention delivery

Compare video and audio formats when an educational or behavior-change intervention depends on belief revision, engagement, or persistence. Preserve an effective audio-accessible path rather than requiring video.

Engagement & communication

Evaluate whether a visible spokesperson changes attention, questions, message acceptance, or follow-through. More objections should not automatically be treated as failure; they may indicate active engagement.

Measurement strategy

Measure questions or protest, belief revision, continuation, and behavioral reliance separately. One satisfaction or trust rating can conceal differences between disagreement, disengagement, and eventual use.

Potential impact measures

Comprehension · belief-updating accuracy · number and type of questions · task completion · follow-through · abandonment or waiting time · perceived social presence · differences across access needs

09 · Limits and next test

What the study establishes—and what it does not

Limitations

The sample was relatively small and largely drawn from White, middle-class families with suitable devices and internet access. Home testing introduced distractions that could not be fully standardized. The design established an effect of channel richness but did not isolate which visual cue produced it. Seven-year-olds also showed a waiting ceiling effect.

Next study

Use a factorial design to vary eye gaze, facial expression, gesture, and perceived social presence independently; recruit a broader age and socioeconomic range; and add post-task questions about how participants interpreted the speaker’s attention and commitment.
